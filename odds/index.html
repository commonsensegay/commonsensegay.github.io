Is somebody juicing it?

Let's be honest, a big bet is not proof of anything by itself. Somebody with money and an opinion is allowed to have both. Here is what would actually point to a market being pushed, and what this page watches for.

  1. A big order moves the price, then it snaps back. If a single order shoves the odds and other traders buy it right back within hours, the market did not believe it.
  2. The same size order, over and over. Repeated orders of nearly identical dollar size look like one player working a position in chunks.
  3. Several markets hit in the same minute. When Texas, Ohio and Senate control all get big orders at once, that is one trader or one bot reacting to one thing. The question is what it reacted to.
  4. The odds move against the data. Odds that drift toward one side while the polls and the early vote stay put deserve a hard look. Odds that move with a new poll or a turnout report are just the market doing its job.
  5. Kalshi disagrees with everyone else. If one exchange is far from the others for days, nobody is arbitraging it, and that is strange.

How this is built. Prices, volume, trades and the order book come from Kalshi's public market data. Daily prices are the last trade of each Eastern time trading day. A big order is every fill of 25,000 or more contracts on the same side in the same second, which is how one large order sweeping through several price levels shows up. Dollar figures are what the taker paid. Kalshi is a CFTC regulated exchange and does not publish who is trading.

What the odds are not. A 64 percent chance is not a 64 percent vote share. A candidate can be the clear betting favorite in a race the polls call a toss-up, because traders are pricing a small lead plus uncertainty. Early vote party splits show who has voted, not how.
